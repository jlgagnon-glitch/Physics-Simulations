About the model

  • Isometric means static. The forearm does not rotate, so the net torque about the elbow is zero and the net force on the forearm is zero.
  • Geometry. 2-D side view of a right arm with the palm up (forearm supinated). Elbow to grip 34 cm, elbow to wrist 26 cm, shoulder 30 cm above the elbow. Each muscle pulls in a straight line from its origin to its insertion.
  • Moment arms come from the attachment points and change with angle. With these attachments they range from 1.4 to 4.4 cm (biceps), 0.7 to 2.3 cm (brachialis), and 1.4 to 6.7 cm (brachioradialis). Those ranges are in line with cadaver measurements (for example, Murray, Delp & Buchanan, 1995).
  • Segments. Forearm 1.10 kg with its center of mass 11 cm from the elbow; hand 0.42 kg at 33 cm (typical values for a 70 kg adult).
  • How the load is shared. Many combinations of three muscle forces balance the same torque, so the model picks the one that keeps every muscle's activation as low as possible: it minimizes the sum of cubed activations (Crowninshield & Brand, 1981), a common way to predict muscle forces. The result: a_i ∝ √(F0i ri), so muscles with more leverage and more available force do more of the work.
  • Why the forces change with angle. Three things change as the elbow bends: the load's torque (W·d·sin φ), each muscle's moment arm, and each muscle's available force F0 = Fmax × fL. The force–length factor fL is greatest when the fibers are at their optimal length (set here at 90° flexion) and falls off when they are stretched or shortened. Maximum forces and optimal fiber lengths are approximate values from a widely used upper-limb model (Holzbaur et al., 2005): biceps 1060 N and 12.4 cm, brachialis 987 N and 8.6 cm, brachioradialis 262 N and 17.3 cm. Tendons are treated as rigid and the triceps as relaxed.
  • The given forces are the model's values rounded to the nearest newton. The answer is calculated from those rounded values, so students' torque balance works out exactly.
